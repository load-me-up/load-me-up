The ones I never named.

1 November 2026

I used to ask their names. It felt like politeness, the done thing, something to say in the gap afterwards. And some men hesitated. Not all of them, but enough that I noticed – a pause before the answer, like I’d asked for something heavier than I meant to. I didn’t understand the hesitation then. I thought a name was just a name.

Reading Andrew McMillan’s “Jacob with the Angel”, of all things, I found myself in it.1 Two men wrestling all night, and in the morning neither of them gives his name, “because names would add a history”. That line sat me down. Not because it was new, but because I’d been living by it for years without ever putting it into words.

It took me a while to understand. A name is a door. Take the men who did tell me theirs – some of them are my friends now, and I don’t sleep with them anymore. I don’t think that’s a coincidence, and I don’t regret it either. Somewhere between “what do I call you?” and the third long conversation, the sex turned into a person, and a person is something you can have feelings for, and feelings are where the trouble starts. I can still touch a man I don’t know. I can’t touch someone I’ve held a jacket for in TK Maxx while he tried things on.

So now I keep the men I shag at a certain distance. I meet men whose names I know, even what they do for a living, and it stays purely that – no drift into each other’s weeks. I meet men who want the anonymity as much as I do: hoods, masks, a blindfold. There are the ones from the lane – a lane with a name so ridiculous I daren’t print it – where we don’t even say hello: “hello” would already be too much biography. Not that it’s silent, there’s a language instead, and a whole ritual: a look held a second too long, a walk that becomes a follow, the pause at the treeline. Sometimes it’s minutes and we’re down the lane before either of us has properly stopped to breathe. Sometimes it takes a few approaches, circling each other through the trees, until eventually I’m bent over a fallen trunk with a man whose name I never wanted and never got. Still no “hello”, though.

There’s the version at home, too. My Twitter bio says “the guy next door. Door’s open” – on the apps, “guy next door” is a tribe, a checkbox you tick, and my bio there is blunter. The kinks list reads: “anon, masked/hooded, being a blindfolded cum dump, tag teaming, DP, outdoors”, and there’s a glory hole available on request, and at the very end of it, the answer to the question half of them ask before anything else: “Discreet if needed.” He messages when he’s parked. The door’s already unlocked, I’m face down on the bed, and we never say a word: the whole conversation happened in typing, before he arrived. A stranger walks into my house, does what he came for, and lets himself out, and the only trace he takes with him is the smell of my house and whatever he saw of the hallway. Discreet if needed – though he never needs to say so.

I want to be honest about this part: anonymous sex has become my kink. The hood, the dark, the man I’ll never be able to place in daylight – I don’t want the kind with talking. Talking builds connections, and I don’t want connections. NSA, as they say. Nothing is being exchanged except bodies. It’s the opposite of a promise, and both of us know it, and it’s a relief, like weather happening.

And none of that means it’s cold. Even the anonymous kind has its warmth: kissing, cuddling, a hooded man’s arm across me afterwards like any other Tuesday. The arrangement screens out names and futures, not tenderness. A man can hold me all night and still never tell me his name.

But here’s the part that makes me laugh at myself: my memory refuses to file any of them under “unknown”. So it invents its own names. The electrician. The guy in the expensive BMW. The lad from my old gym. I never asked for their names and yet I’ve named every one of them – I just chose names that carry no history. A handle, not an address. “Electrician” tells me what he does to the lights, not who he is to me. Somewhere between “electrician” and “Daniel” is the exact point where a shag becomes a person.

The phone did the same thing twice over. I used to swap numbers, chat on WhatsApp – and after a while, one of us would go quiet. Sometimes they ghosted me, sometimes, if I’m honest, I was the ghost. That’s the full cost of a name, I think: not that it lets someone in, but that it gives leaving somewhere to land. The electricians can’t ghost me. They can only stop appearing, which is weather. A man with a name can leave, and it hurts, and it hurts precisely because a name made him a person before he went. The apps fixed this the way I fix everything: handles instead of names, private notes so I don’t mix them up – “roofer, can host in the van” – small, silly acts of ink to keep them alive without letting them be known.

The strange thing is what’s left of them now. The names I did have, I’ve mostly lost. Faces too. What survives is the body’s evidence: nice hands, a smooth arse, long hair I wrapped a fist in, a smell that comes back at four in the morning. Not a person, more a flashback. I’ve realised the bodies were the real record all along; everything else was administration. I wouldn’t recognise some of my regulars in Sainsbury’s, standing there with the wife and kids, staring at the ready meals – men who have been inside me more times than I can count. We were never introduced. That was the arrangement.

I say all this like a policy, so let me be honest about where the policy leaks. There are a few men I share a bit of my life with, and it’s exactly the ones who wouldn’t care if I stopped – and I wouldn’t much care if they did. I only open the door where I’ve checked there’s nothing behind it worth losing. If that sounds cold, I can hear it too. I’d rather call it knowing what I can carry.

Because the protection works. It does. The men I never named don’t reach me. But in “A Gift”, McMillan made a list of every man he never touched, every one who got away, and I noticed he could only grieve them as “the one who served me coffee”, “the ones who wanted to watch films”. Categories, like mine. They got into his history anyway, all of them – the rule only made them untraceable. He wrote that they were young, and they only had their bodies. It’s meant as grief, and it reads like grief: the receipt for a filing system just like mine.2

And some nights I catch the leak in my own system. I think about the men whose names I’ve forgotten and wonder how they’re doing now. Whether they still have that car, that hair, that smell. Whether any of them ever lies awake and wonders about me – whether I’m a flashback in someone else’s body, a bit of evidence the night left behind.

It would be very nice to know. I never will.

I used to ask their names.

– Anon.
